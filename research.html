Research

Findings that came out of building this simulator. Several were surprises, and a couple overturned earlier conclusions of our own.

Methodology

Reference bandwidth is a first-class benchmark parameter

Motion benchmarks record ground truth with motion-capture or total-station rigs, and everyone treats that reference as effectively exact. Measured spectrally, the reference orientation on the aggressive-flight sequences we examined is heavily low-pass filtered — retaining almost nothing above 15 Hz — while the vehicle genuinely carries angular-rate content well beyond that, and the camera is evaluated at 30 Hz.

Because both the camera and the gyroscope observe the true motion but are scored against a smoothed reference, the discarded motion becomes a common error injected into both. In controlled simulation, degrading an exact reference reproduces the effect precisely.

Two things follow. Apparent sensor error inflates by up to 10× at aggressive rates. And a bandwidth-limited reference manufactures correlation between sensors whose errors are genuinely independent — with an exact reference the error vectors are 83° apart, which is orthogonal; at 2 Hz they collapse to 28°.

That second effect is the damaging one, because it silently caps measured sensor-fusion gain. A fusion study run against such a reference will conclude fusion does not help, when the underlying errors were independent all along.

Practice

The fisheye trap

Racing drones fly wide lenses. The sensor modelled here is equidistant with a ~115° horizontal field, and its calibration file says so in plain text. It is nevertheless very easy to feed raw pixels through a pinhole intrinsic — the code runs, the numbers look plausible, and everything downstream is wrong.

We made exactly this mistake in our own earlier analysis. Correcting it reduced measured visual rotation error by 61–73%, and cut the gyroscope's apparent advantage over vision from 2.9× to 1.17×. The conclusion survived; every number supporting it did not.

Under an equidistant model image radius grows as f·θ, while a pinhole assumes f·tan θ. The two diverge sharply toward the edge of a wide field.

Hardware

In-flight gyroscope noise is ~9× the datasheet

Calibrating the simulator against recorded flight required a gyroscope noise density of about 0.043 rad/s/√Hz to reproduce measured error growth. The part is specified at 0.005. The gap is almost certainly airframe vibration coupling into the sensor.

The practical consequence: simulate against datasheet figures and every inertial result you produce will be optimistic by roughly an order of magnitude in noise power.

Negative results

Things we tried that did not work

Published in the same spirit as the rest. Each was killed by a cheap, decisive test rather than months of development, which is the main argument for having a simulator at all.

Depth from motion blur

Blur extent encodes inverse depth, and the IMU supplies the blur direction, so only the length is unknown. Four estimators — cepstral, deconvolution sharpness, gradient kurtosis, spectral signature — all failed to recover blur length usably at realistic magnitudes of 3–13 px. Coded exposure improved kernel conditioning as theory predicts (destroyed frequency bins fell from 32/65 to 10/65) but did not rescue the estimator.

Wide-baseline "sweep stereo"

A spinning drone re-points at the same direction after translating metres, which looks like free wide-baseline stereo. Co-visibility collapses first: verified feature matches fell to zero beyond roughly a 1 m baseline, and at racing speed even the shortest revisit interval already exceeds that.

A negative result with a diagnosed mechanism is worth more than a positive one without.
