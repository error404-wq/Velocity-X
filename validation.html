Validation

A simulator is only helpful if you know exactly how accurate it is. This page explains what the simulator gets perfectly right, and where it relies on approximations.

The principle: Everything related to geometry and physics is perfectly exact. Everything related to the camera and sensors is a very close, highly calibrated approximation.

1 · Geometry is exact

Scenes are built from flat walls and floors. Because we use flat surfaces, we can mathematically calculate the exact depth for every single pixel without using rendering shortcuts like depth buffers.

We don't just take that on faith. Our testing suite renders a scene, takes the generated depth, and traces it back into the 3D world to prove that it lands exactly on the correct wall.

4.4×10⁻¹⁶ is double-precision epsilon at metre scale. The geometry is exact to the limits of the number format.

2 · Flight paths are mathematically stable

When you tell the drone to spin, we calculate its new orientation using proper 3D rotation math. This prevents the simulator from slowly drifting out of alignment over long flights — a common bug in many custom simulation tools. Our math is verified to remain perfectly stable.

Self-consistency was confirmed by comparing the integrated trajectory against the analytic one, and by checking that the residual falls with step size — the signature of discretisation rather than a defect.

3 · Inertial noise is calibrated to real flight, not the datasheet

The obvious thing to do is take the gyroscope noise density from the IMU datasheet. On a racing drone that is optimistic by nearly an order of magnitude, because the airframe vibrates.

Measured against recorded flight, the effective in-flight noise density is ≈0.043 rad/s/√Hz — about 9× the 0.005 quoted for the part. The simulator uses the measured figure. Using the datasheet would make every result that depends on inertial accuracy quietly optimistic.

4 · Motion blur is integrated, not convolved

Under fast rotation, blur is not a single kernel applied to the whole image: the smear direction and length differ at every pixel because projective flow does. The simulator renders true sub-exposures across the shutter interval and integrates them, which reproduces that spatial variation by construction.

5 · Where it should not be trusted

Every simulator has a boundary. Being explicit about this one is more useful than claiming it does not exist.

Event model is uncalibrated

Event generation uses a standard log-intensity threshold model with plausible parameters, but those parameters have not been fitted to a real event sensor — particularly its low-light behaviour. Any conclusion whose answer depends on the event threshold model is an assumption, not a measurement.

Scenes are piecewise-planar

Exact depth is bought with planar geometry. There are no curved surfaces, no non-Lambertian reflectance, no lighting changes, and no independently moving objects. Texture is synthetic with natural spectral statistics, not photographs.

Absolute visual error is optimistic

Rendered images lack the full range of real-world nuisance: rolling shutter, compression, auto-exposure transients, lens flare. Expect simulated feature tracking to look somewhat better than reality.

It complements real data, it does not replace it

The right workflow is to develop and ablate here, where causes are separable, then confirm on recorded flight. A result that appears only in simulation is a hypothesis.
